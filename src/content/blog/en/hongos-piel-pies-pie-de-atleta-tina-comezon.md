# Skin and Foot Fungus: Athlete's Foot, Ringworm and the Itch That Won't Quit

Twelve hours inside steel-toe boots, a full shift standing by the fryer, or the shared shower at the apartment complex gym. In Houston, where the humidity hangs on even in November, feet spend much of the day hot and damp with sweat. That is exactly the environment skin fungus looks for.

Many patients walk in saying "it's just an itch that won't go away." Sometimes they have been scratching between their toes or in the groin for months, or they notice a round patch on the arm that slowly grows. Here is a look at what athlete's foot and ringworm are, how they spread and when it no longer makes sense to put up with them, based on general information from the [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/ringworm/) and [MedlinePlus](https://medlineplus.gov/athletesfoot.html).

## What a skin fungus is

The fungi behind these infections live in the outermost layer of the skin, in nails and in hair. They feed on keratin, the protein those layers are made of, and they grow best where there is warmth, moisture and friction. It has nothing to do with being unclean: anyone can get it, even with a daily shower.

Despite the name, ringworm has no worm involved. It is the same kind of fungus, and the name changes with the part of the body:

| Where it shows up | Common name | What it usually looks like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Between the toes and on the sole | Athlete's foot | White, soggy skin, cracks, peeling, burning |
| Arms, legs, trunk | Ringworm of the body | Round patch with an itchy red edge and a clearer center |
| Groin and inner thighs | Jock itch | Itchy red area that creeps toward the thighs |
| Toenails | Nail fungus | Yellowish, thick nail that crumbles or lifts |

One person can have more than one. It is common for the fungus to start on the feet and then show up in the groin: it travels on the hands or on the same towel.

## How it spreads

According to the CDC, these fungi pass along in several ways:

- **Wet floors:** showers at gyms, pools, locker rooms and shared bathrooms.
- **Personal items:** another person's towels, socks, shoes, flip-flops, razors or clothes.
- **Direct contact:** skin to skin, for example in contact sports or sharing a bed.
- **Pets:** dogs and cats, especially puppies and kittens, can carry ringworm and pass it on when you hold them. Sometimes it shows on the animal as a bald patch.
- **Your own body:** scratching your feet and then your groin or neck moves the fungus from one spot to another.

Sweat trapped inside closed shoes does the rest. That is why it comes up so often in people who work in construction, landscaping, warehouses or kitchens, and in anyone who wears the same sneakers every day.

## Preventing it day to day

Prevention is more about moisture than about soap:

1. **Dry carefully between your toes** after bathing, one by one. It is the step almost everyone skips.
2. **Change your socks** when they get damp, and if you sweat a lot, bring a spare pair to work. Cotton or moisture-wicking socks help.
3. **Rotate your shoes:** if you can, avoid wearing the same boots two days in a row so they dry out inside.
4. **Wear flip-flops** in shared showers, locker rooms and around the pool.
5. **Do not share** towels, socks, shoes or razors, not even at home.
6. **Put your socks on before your underwear** if you already have athlete's foot, so you do not drag it up to the groin.
7. **Wash towels and bedding** in hot water if someone at home has the infection.
8. **Check your pet** if bald patches appear, and take it to the vet.

## Common mistakes at home

Some habits drag the problem out:

- Scratching hard: it breaks the skin and opens the door to bacteria.
- Using whatever a relative had left over "because it was for itching too." Not every itchy patch is fungus, and not every product does the same job.
- Harsh home remedies such as bleach or rubbing alcohol, which irritate and crack the skin further.
- Stopping care as soon as the itch stops. The fungus may linger under the surface and come back.

If you already tried something on your own, snap a photo of the box or bring it to the visit. It helps to know what has been used.

## Not everything that itches is fungus

Skin has only a few ways to complain: redness, itching, flaking. So a patch that looks like ringworm can be something else, such as an allergic reaction, eczema, irritation from a product or a bacterial infection. And the other way around: a fungus is sometimes mistaken for "dry skin" for months. If the itch comes with hives that appear and fade, or gets worse after touching something specific, the [allergy](/services/alergias) service looks at both possibilities.

## When a clinic visit makes sense

Come in for a general medicine visit if:

- The itch or patch does not improve with home care, or keeps coming back.
- The patch spreads, new ones appear or it moves to another part of the body.
- There are cracks between the toes that hurt, bleed or ooze.
- Your toenails look thick, yellow or are lifting off.
- The skin is very inflamed, warm or blistered.
- You have diabetes, poor circulation or a weakened immune system from an illness or a treatment.

At the visit, the medical team at Clínica Hispana La Caridad looks at the skin, asks when it started, what work you do, whether there are pets at home and what you have put on it, and explains what it looks like and how to treat it. If the picture is unclear or does not respond, the team will tell you whether specialized evaluation is worthwhile.

## With diabetes, feet come first

With diabetes, a crack between the toes is not a small thing. Feeling in the feet may be reduced, so a wound hurts little and gets noticed late, and skin broken open by fungus lets bacteria in. The clinic's [wound care](/services/curacion-heridas) page puts it plainly: a foot wound in a person with diabetes always gets checked, even if it looks small.

Look at your feet every night, with a mirror if needed, and do not wait for it to "heal by itself." If blood sugar is also out of control, the skin defends itself less well; glucose and A1C follow-up is handled under [chronic conditions](/services/condiciones-cronicas).

## Warning signs: 911 or the ER

A fungal infection is rarely an emergency, but broken skin can pick up a bacterial infection. Any of these signs calls for the ER or a 911 call, not a clinic visit:

- The skin turns red, warm and swollen and the redness spreads fast, with fever or chills.
- Red streaks run up the leg from the foot.
- The area darkens or turns black.
- There is confusion, marked weakness or you suddenly feel very ill.
