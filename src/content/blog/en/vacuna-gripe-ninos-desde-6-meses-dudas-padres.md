# Flu Shots for Kids: From 6 Months Old, and the Questions Parents Ask

In November, in southwest Houston, daycares are already sending feverish kids home and families are planning Thanksgiving dinner with the grandparents. And in the waiting room, the yearly question comes back: "Does my child really need the flu shot? We gave it to him last year and he got sick anyway."

It is a fair question. Here are the answers, based on general [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/flu/) information and on what is done at Clínica Hispana La Caridad.

## How young can a child be vaccinated?

The CDC recommends a flu vaccine every season for everyone 6 months and older, with rare exceptions. At the clinic, the [vaccines](/services/vacunas) service gives the influenza vaccine starting at exactly that age, walk-in.

What about a baby who is 3 or 4 months old? Before 6 months the vaccine does not work well in a baby's body, so it is not given. That baby's protection depends on the people around them: if the parents, the siblings, the grandmother who babysits and the aunt who holds the baby are all vaccinated, the virus is less likely to reach the crib. If you are pregnant, bring up the flu shot at your visit: according to the CDC, the vaccine a mother receives during pregnancy also protects the baby in the first months of life.

## "Can the shot give my child the flu?"

No. The injectable vaccine is made with inactivated virus or with a single protein from the virus, so there is nothing in it that can cause the illness. Why, then, are so many parents sure their child got sick right afterwards? There are three common explanations:

- **The body needs about two weeks to build its defenses.** If your child caught the virus during that gap, or a few days before the shot, the flu still comes.
- **Fall brings many other viruses.** Common colds, throat bugs and others that cause a runny nose and cough. The flu vaccine does not protect against those, and at home they all get called "the flu."
- **Mild vaccine effects can look like a cold.** A little fever and low energy for a day or two is the immune system responding, not the disease.

## What is normal after the shot

Most kids end up with nothing more than a sore arm or leg. What you can expect, usually gone within a day or two:

- Pain, redness or a small swelling where the shot went in
- Low-grade fever
- Headache or body aches in older children
- More fussiness or sleepiness in little ones

Offer fluids, let your child rest and keep an eye on the spot. If the fever or discomfort worries you, ask the clinic before giving any medicine. Severe allergic reactions are very rare and show up within minutes to hours: swelling of the face or lips, hives all over the body or trouble breathing. If that happens, call 911.

Egg allergy? According to the CDC it does not rule out the vaccine; still, mention any earlier reaction to a shot.

## Why every year and not just once

With influenza, two things happen:

1. **The virus changes.** Slightly different strains circulate each season, and the vaccine is updated to match the ones expected that winter.
2. **Protection fades over the months.** Whatever last year's shot left behind is no longer enough for this season.

Ideally it goes in between September and October, but it keeps helping after that: if your child still lacks it in November, it is worth getting.

## Why some young children need two doses

According to the CDC, children from 6 months through 8 years old who are getting a flu vaccine for the first time, or who have only had one dose in total before, need **two doses given at least four weeks apart** that season. The first dose "introduces" the virus to their defenses and the second one gets them ready. With only one, a child is half protected that first year.

From the second year on, or from age 9, one dose per season is enough. That is why it helps to bring the vaccination card, even one from another country: the clinic's medical team reviews the record and tells you whether your child needs one dose or two.

## Children with asthma, diabetes or other chronic conditions

For these kids the vaccine matters even more. The CDC notes that flu can turn serious more easily in children under 5, especially under 2, and in children of any age with:

- Asthma or another lung condition
- Diabetes
- Heart or kidney disease
- Neurological or developmental conditions
- A weakened immune system from an illness or a treatment

In a child with asthma, influenza can set off an attack that lands them in the hospital. Asthma is no reason to skip the shot; it is a reason not to put it off.

## If your child is already sick

With a plain cold and no fever, a child can usually be vaccinated. If there is a fever or your child seems run down, the vaccines page asks you to say so beforehand: your child is assessed first and the shot waits until they recover.

If you suspect it is already the flu (a fever that came on suddenly, body aches, cough, real exhaustion), do not wait for it to pass. The [respiratory illness](/services/enfermedades-respiratorias) service runs the rapid influenza test, with results in minutes. If your child has asthma or a chronic condition, speed counts more: an antiviral does its best work when begun in the first two days of symptoms. If treatment is needed, you can buy what the clinic's medical team prescribed right there at the [clinic pharmacy](/services/farmacia), and they explain in Spanish how to give it.

At home: plenty of fluids, rest, and no aspirin for children or teenagers. Before going back to school or daycare, wait until your child has gone a full day without fever without fever-reducing medicine and the other symptoms are getting better.

## Warning signs: 911 or the emergency room

Do not wait until morning: 911 or the ER when your child:

- Breathes fast or with effort, or the ribs pull in with each breath
- Has bluish lips or face
- Has not urinated in eight hours, has a dry mouth or cries without tears
- Is hard to wake, does not respond or does not want to be held
- Has a seizure
- Has a fever and is a baby younger than 12 weeks
- Got better and then the fever or cough came back stronger
- Has asthma or a chronic condition that is clearly getting worse
