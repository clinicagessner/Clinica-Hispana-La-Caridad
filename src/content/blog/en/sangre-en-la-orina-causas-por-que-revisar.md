# Blood in Your Urine: Possible Causes and Why It Always Gets Checked

It often goes like this. You flush in the morning, notice the water in the bowl looked pinkish, and your stomach drops. An hour later the urine is clear again. Nothing hurts, it seems to have sorted itself out, so you let it go. Other people never see anything at all: they find out because a dipstick at a work physical or a yearly check-up came back positive for blood.

Either way, the takeaway is the same: **blood in the urine deserves a proper look, even when it is painless, even if it happened once, and even after it has cleared**. Most of the time the cause turns out to be minor. The point is that nobody can tell where it is coming from without looking for it.

## Seen or found: the two kinds

Health professionals call it **hematuria** and sort it into two groups:

- **Gross (visible):** the urine looks pink, red, tea-colored or has dark streaks. A tiny amount of blood is enough to change the color, so how alarming it looks says little about how much there is.
- **Microscopic:** the urine looks perfectly normal, but red blood cells show up on testing. It is a common finding on routine labs and causes no symptoms.

Neither one is automatically the milder kind. Microscopic blood that keeps showing up on repeat tests is worked up just as carefully as urine that turned red.

## Red does not always mean blood

Before thinking about causes, it helps to know that several everyday things tint urine with no blood involved:

| What colors it | Typical shade | What to do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Beets, blackberries, rhubarb | Pink or reddish | Note what you ate the day before |
| Dyes in candy and drinks | Reddish or orange | See whether it returns once you stop |
| Some medicines and urinary pain relievers | Red, orange or brown | Bring a list of what you take |
| Heavy dehydration | Very dark amber | Drink water; if it stays dark, come in |
| A period or vaginal bleeding | Pink in the sample | Mention it when you give the sample |

The urine test is what settles it: the dipstick and microscope show whether there are real red blood cells or only pigment. So even if you are fairly sure it was the beet salad, it is worth confirming.

## Frequent, treatable explanations

When there really is blood, the reason is often common and manageable:

- **A urinary tract infection.** An irritated bladder lining can bleed, sometimes with burning or urgency and sometimes with hardly any discomfort. This is one of the causes seen most often.
- **Stones in the urinary tract.** A stone scraping along the inside can bleed, with or without the classic sharp pain.
- **Very hard exercise or long physical strain.** Distance running, heavy lifting or a punishing shift of manual work can leave a little blood that fades within a day or two.
- **A contaminated sample.** During or close to a period, or with any vaginal bleeding, blood can mix into the urine as it is collected. That is not a urinary problem, but the test has to be repeated another time.
- **A recent blow** to the lower back or side.
- **Blood thinners.** They can make a small bleed easier to notice, though on their own they are rarely the whole story, which is why the source is still looked for.

## Causes that call for a closer look

Less often, blood is the first clue to something that needs a deeper workup:

- An enlarged prostate in older men.
- Inflammation of the kidney's filters, sometimes with protein in the urine, swelling or high blood pressure.
- Kidney conditions that run in families.
- And, in a smaller number of people, a tumor in the bladder or kidney.

That last point is not meant to frighten anyone: most people with blood in their urine do not have cancer. Still, painless visible blood is one of the few early signals some urinary tract tumors give, and that is exactly why it is never brushed off. The risk is higher in current or former smokers, in people over 50, and in those who spent years around dyes, solvents or industrial chemicals. If any of that applies to you, say so at the visit; it shapes the next step.

## "It already went away" is not the end of it

The most common mistake is waiting to see if it happens again. Hematuria can show up, vanish for weeks and come back, and whatever is causing it is still there in between. Clear urine today tells you nothing about the source. If you saw blood even once, get it checked within the next few days rather than waiting for a repeat.

The same goes for a lab result: if a work physical flagged red blood cells, keep that sheet and bring it. It is useful information, not paperwork.

## Giving a good sample

Small details that save you from repeating the test:

- Wash your hands and clean the area before you start.
- Pass the first few drops into the toilet, then fill the cup from midstream.
- If you are on your period or it is about to start, mention it; sometimes it is better to give the sample again a few days later.
- Skip intense exercise the day before a follow-up test.
- Bring a list of your medicines and supplements, including any blood thinners.

## Signs that cannot wait for an appointment: ER or 911

Do not wait for a regular visit if the blood comes with:

- **Clots**, or urine that is deep red, like cherry juice.
- **Being unable to pass any urine**, with a full, painful bladder.
- **A high fever and shaking chills together with flank or lower-back pain.**
- **Feeling lightheaded or passing out**, looking very pale, or throwing up so much that even water will not stay down.
- A hard hit to the back or belly followed by blood when you urinate.

Any of these means heading to the emergency room or calling 911 instead of booking a visit.
